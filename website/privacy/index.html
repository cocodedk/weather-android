Privacy policy: Weather

Effective 7 October 2026. This policy covers the Weather app for Android (app ID dk.cocode.weather).

Summary

Weather has no account, no ads and no analytics (tools that track how people use an app). There is no server of ours behind it. Cocode, who makes the app, receives nothing from it.

To show a forecast, the app asks Open-Meteo, a free weather service run by someone else. So Open-Meteo learns which places you view and search for.

Using your location is optional. If you tap "Use my current location", the app also gives an approximate position to a place-name service built into your phone.

Everything else stays on your phone.

What is collected

Cocode collects no personal data through the app. The app has no sign-up, and no server of ours to send anything to. The only web addresses it calls are the two Open-Meteo ones in the next section. This is what the app sends or uses:

  • What you type in the search box. Once you have typed two characters, the app sends the text to Open-Meteo to find matching places. This is the only time the app sends anything you type.
  • The coordinates of the place you are viewing. Coordinates are two numbers, latitude and longitude, that mark a point on the map. The place can be one you searched for, one you saved, or one found from your location. The app sends its coordinates to Open-Meteo, rounded to four decimals, to get its forecast.
  • Your approximate location, only if you ask. The app uses it once each time you tap "Use my current location", to find your place. See Permissions.
  • Your phone's language code, such as en or da. It goes along with a search, so place names come back in your language.
  • Your IP address and the time of each request. An IP address is a number that identifies your internet connection. The app connects straight to Open-Meteo, so Open-Meteo can see both, as any website you visit can.

The app adds no account, key or device identifier (a number that identifies your phone) to any request.

Servers the app contacts

The app contacts two servers. Both are run by Open-Meteo, and both use HTTPS, an encrypted connection. The app contacts no other server and opens no links.

api.open-meteo.com: forecasts

This server sends back the current weather, the hour-by-hour outlook and the seven-day forecast.

The app sends the latitude and longitude of the selected place, rounded to four decimals, and a list of the weather values it wants, such as temperature, wind and precipitation. It also sends a User-Agent, a short label that says which app is asking: dk.cocode.weather/1.0 (Android). Open-Meteo asks apps to send one.

The app asks for a forecast when it starts, when you pick a place, and when you choose Refresh. To find Refresh, tap the "More" icon at the top of the screen.

If you have added the Weather widget to your home screen (a small panel that shows the weather without opening the app), the widget asks too. It asks about every 30 minutes, when you tap its refresh icon, and when you change the place or the units in the app. Android decides the exact timing of the 30-minute updates.

geocoding-api.open-meteo.com: place search

This server finds places by name. The app sends the text you typed, once you pause typing for a moment, and your phone's language code.

Nothing from a search is saved unless you pick a place from the results. Picking one adds it to your saved places.

What Open-Meteo does with it

Open-Meteo is an independent service. The app cannot see or control what Open-Meteo keeps. Its terms and privacy notice are at open-meteo.com/en/terms.

Android's place-name service (not a server of the app)

When you use your current location, the app gives your position to Geocoder. Geocoder is a service built into Android that turns a position into a place name. It belongs to your phone, not to the app.

The app cannot tell which company's service your phone uses, or where that service sends the position. Some phones have none. If the service is missing or fails, the app shows the coordinates instead of a name.

Permissions

A permission lets an app use a feature of your phone. The app asks for three:

  • Internet. Lets the app reach the two Open-Meteo servers above.
  • Network state. Lets an app see whether the phone is online. The app lists this permission, but its own code does not use it.
  • Approximate location. Optional. The app asks for it only when you tap "Use my current location". It asks for approximate location, not precise location, and never for location while you are not using the app.

If you say no to location, search still works. If you say yes, each tap makes the app ask Android for a single position: a recent one Android already has, or a fresh one. Then the app stops asking.

The widget and the app's other forecast requests never read your location. They use the place saved the last time you tapped.

The app asks for no other permissions.

What stays on the phone

The app keeps the following in its own private storage on your phone:

  • Your saved places: name, country, region, coordinates and time zone. Every place you pick from a search is saved. The app starts with Copenhagen saved. A place found from your location is saved with its coordinates and replaced by the next one, so they do not pile up.
  • Which place is selected, whether you use metric or imperial units, and your theme (day, night or automatic).
  • The last forecast the app received for each place, so it can show something when you are offline.

To remove a saved place:

  1. Tap the place name at the top of the screen, or the "Change location" icon. The Locations list opens.
  2. Tap the delete icon (a bin) next to the place. The place disappears from the list.

The delete icon appears only while you have more than one place saved. The app does not delete the saved forecast of a place you remove.

To erase everything the app keeps, clear the app's data in Android's settings, or uninstall the app. Either one removes all your saved places and settings.

Android can back up your phone's data. The app allows this for its own files and excludes nothing. If backup is switched on for your phone, these files can be part of it. Your phone, not the app, decides where that backup is stored.

Third parties

A third party is a company or service other than you and Cocode. Two are involved:

  • Open-Meteo provides the forecasts and the place search, as described above.
  • Android's Geocoder names your location, as described above. It is part of your phone.

The app has no analytics, no ads and no crash reporting (automatic error reports), and it does not use Google Play services. Its build file lists none of them. Its libraries (ready-made code from others) are AndroidX libraries for drawing the screen, handling the app starting and stopping, and storing data on the phone. The app sends its requests with the web-request code that comes with Android, not with a separate networking library.

The website is separate. This policy is about the app. On weather.cocode.dk, the live forecast panel sends what you search for, and the coordinates of the place it shows, to Open-Meteo from your browser. It also keeps the last forecast in your browser's local storage. The pages load fonts from Google Fonts and the shared cocode.dk header and footer from brand.cocode.dk, and GitHub Pages serves them.

Your rights

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is the European privacy law. Under it you can ask to see your data, ask for it to be corrected or deleted, and ask us to limit how it is used.

The app sends nothing to Cocode, so Cocode holds no personal data about you from the app. There is nothing for us to show, correct or delete. You can remove what the app keeps on your phone yourself, as described above.

Open-Meteo holds the data that reached it, so you take up these rights with Open-Meteo. You can also complain to the Danish Data Protection Agency, Datatilsynet.
